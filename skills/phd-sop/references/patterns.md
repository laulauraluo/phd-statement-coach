# 15 patterns from admitted PhD statements

Distilled from 21 admitted CS, ML and robotics PhD statements in the public library at https://cs-sop.notion.site (paraphrased; no text copied) and from published advice: the CMU Student Academic Success Center SOP handout, Mor Harchol-Balter's grad-school talk, Andy Pavlo's "How to write a bad statement", the MIT EECS Communication Lab, Noah Smith's PhD application FAQ, Nelson Liu et al., Maria Antoniak and Saujas Vaduguru. These are survivors' essays, so no pattern is proven to cause admission; several admits also had records strong enough to carry a weak essay.

Use each pattern as a check: pass, partial or fail, with one line of evidence from the draft.

1. **Case, point, question within about 100 words.** Open with one or two concrete cases, state the general problem they share, then the research question you would start on tomorrow. A personal origin, if used, is 1 to 3 sentences and turns straight to research.
2. **Use the target field's words, and say where your angle differs.** One clause that places the question in its research neighborhood (for robotics: active perception, belief-space planning, value of information, and so on) heads off the worry that the applicant is rediscovering it. Use only terms the applicant can defend in an interview.
3. **One thread, every project an honest instance of it.** Do not stretch the thread to cover projects it does not fit; reviewers who open the papers will notice. If one project fits differently, say how.
4. **One shape per project:** problem, why current methods fail, what the applicant did, a number against a named baseline, a one-line lesson, a bridge to the next part.
5. **Causal transitions.** Each paragraph's first sentence picks up the last one ("This raised a question", "Checking X exposed Y"). Keep the real chronological order.
6. **State ownership.** "My main contribution was..." or one line on the applicant's role, separated from the team's. Match any AI-use or contribution statements in the papers.
7. **One sharp number per major project, with its condition and baseline.** Not "up to 99%" when the result is a range under a condition.
8. **Industry is where research happened.** Problem, method, evaluation, lesson; no apology for the industry years. One technical clause per role beats a list of titles.
9. **Name the gap or setback once, then what you did about it.** Changing fields, no hands-on experience yet, a failed result: one plain sentence, then the action. Reporting a negative result is evidence of integrity.
10. **The plan grows from a limit of the applicant's own work,** with a first experiment and an exact way to score it.
11. **Faculty: 2 or 3 names, each tied to one specific paper and to what the applicant's question adds.** Weakest: names listed as "aligned". Strongest: an extension of a named method, or a real existing tie. Never invent contact. Rewrite only the fit and closing paragraphs per school.
12. **Close by echoing the opening;** one sentence on the goal after the PhD and how the degree gets there. Closings are where most admitted statements are weakest.
13. **Plain, specific wording.** Evidence instead of adjectives; define each technical term inline once; no hedges after results; no em dashes.
14. **Length and shape.** Bodies cluster at about 950 to 1,300 words; one message per paragraph; follow the program's prompt order. Bold run-in heads are optional.
15. **Personal parts: one concrete scene that produces the motive,** actions instead of trait claims ("persistent", "passionate"), no repetition of the research argument.

## What not to copy

- Openings built on credentials (job titles, publication counts, awards). They only work with that kind of record.
- A self-promoting tone ("having me would expand your influence").
- No fit paragraph, or a list of names.
- Hedges after results ("promising results") and generic closings ("ideal environment", "I belong").

## On flow

Statements that read smoothly get it from structure, not adjectives: causal openers, one thread phrase repeated in the same words, and a lesson sentence that ends each project and sets up the next.
